Metropolis–Hastings

Description

The Metropolis–Hastings algorithm is a Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) method used to generate samples from complex probability distributions where direct sampling is difficult. It works by proposing new points based on a jump distribution and accepting or rejecting them according to how well they match the target distribution.

While powerful and flexible, it can suffer from slow convergence or poor mixing when the proposal distribution is not well-tuned. It's commonly used in Bayesian inference, computational physics, and population genetics.

Algorithm

Algorithm Details

The Metropolis–Hastings algorithm constructs a Markov chain whose equilibrium distribution matches a chosen target probability function p(θ). Each iteration proposes a new candidate state and decides whether to move there or remain at the current position.

1. Probability Function p(θ)

The probability function p(θ) defines the landscape that the algorithm explores. It may represent a likelihood, posterior, or any function proportional to them. The algorithm does not require p(θ) to be normalized—only ratios such as p(θ′) / p(θ) are needed. This makes the method especially useful in Bayesian inference, where normalizing constants are often intractable.

2. Jumping Function J(θ′ | θ)

The jumping function (or proposal distribution) J(θ′ | θ) determines how new candidate points are generated from the current state. A common choice is a symmetric Gaussian centered at the current point, for example θ′ = θ + Normal(0, σ²), but any distribution can be used.

The form of J controls how efficiently the algorithm explores the space: smaller jumps produce higher acceptance but slower movement, while larger jumps cover ground faster but are rejected more frequently.

3. Acceptance Probability

The acceptance probability determines whether a proposed move is accepted or rejected. It is given by line 2 of the while-loop shown above, where the ratio of probabilities and proposal terms decides if the chain moves to the new point θ′ or remains at θ.

4. Convergence

Convergence refers to the stage when the distribution of visited states becomes indistinguishable from the true target distribution p(θ). At this point, samples can be treated as representative draws from that distribution.

In practice, convergence is evaluated through diagnostics such as effective sample size, autocorrelation, or the stability of running means. Because the early part of the chain is often still adapting, a burn-in period is usually discarded before analysis.

Summary

Together, the probability function p(θ), the jumping function J(θ′ | θ), and the acceptance rule (line 2) form the foundation of the Metropolis–Hastings algorithm. These components create a stochastic process that not only identifies high-probability regions, but also characterizes the entire landscape of uncertainty surrounding them.
